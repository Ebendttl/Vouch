Prove one thing. Black out the rest.

Vouch lets the holder of an encrypted Avalanche balance prove a single fact about it — to one named counterparty, once — without revealing the balance and without handing anyone an auditor key.

Statement presented to 0xBob — lender

subject0x7a3f…c219 · Alice Okonkwo
balance14,208.55 USDC
history62 transfers · 9 counterparties
positionseERC #1, #4, #7
balance ≥ 1,000 USDC verified on chain

Bob learns that one line. Not the balance, not the history, not tomorrow’s balance. Carol replaying Bob’s proof is rejected.

eERC has two disclosure modes. Finance needs three.

Avalanche’s Encrypted ERC is the strongest confidential-token primitive in production on any EVM chain. It encrypts balances and transfer amounts while keeping a path open for compliance. But it offers an account exactly two settings:

ModeWho can seeHow much
DefaultNobodyNothing
AuditorManagerOne designated auditorEverything, forever
VouchOne named counterpartyOne fact, once, until an expiry

The consequence is sharper than it looks. The moment an encrypted balance has to deal with anyone who is not that auditor — a lender, an exchange, a landlord, an OTC desk, a regulator in a second jurisdiction, a grants committee — the holder chooses between revealing nothing and handing over the master key.

Which is why nearly every eERC project so far is payroll. Payroll is the one case where the payer already knows the amount.

Everything else in finance runs on proving something to someone who should not see everything. Confidential money cannot participate in an economy it cannot prove anything to.

How a statement works

  1. The parties agree terms off chainSubject, verifier, token, threshold, expiry, one-time nonce. Six fields, hashed together with the chain id into a binding.
  2. The subject proves it locallyA Groth16 proof that their eERC balance clears the threshold. The key and the balance never leave their machine.
  3. The verifier presents itOne transaction, paid by the party who wants the answer. The contract checks the proof against the ciphertext currently on chain and returns a single bit.

Change any term and the proof fails. Present it from a different address and it fails. Present it twice and it fails. Present it after the expiry and it fails.

Why this is a small build, not a moonshot

The hard cryptography already exists on Avalanche and has already been audited. eERC’s Withdraw circuit already proves balance ≥ amount — it has to, before it will let you move funds. It simply couples that fact to a state change and then discards it.

// circuits/balance_at_least.circom

// 1. the prover controls the registered account
component pk = BabyPbk();
pk.in <== senderPrivateKey;
pk.Ax === senderPublicKey[0];

// 2. correct ElGamal decryption:  b·G == C2 − sk·C1
balPoint.Ax === diff.xout;

// 3. the assertion
component ge = GreaterEqThan(128);
ge.in[0] <== senderBalance;
ge.in[1] <== threshold;
ge.out === 1;

// 4. bound to one verifier, one deadline, one nonce
contextSquared <== contextHash * contextHash;

Same curve, same proving system, same decryption check, same comparator — with the state change removed and a binding added. Ninety lines. The primitive Avalanche needs is sitting inside a circuit Avalanche already shipped. Nobody has pulled it out.

Statement types

TypeClaimStatus
BalanceAtLeastBalance is at least TWorking
RangeBandBalance sits in [A, B]Two comparators away
FlowAtLeastReceived at least T over N daysRoadmap — needs an accumulator
FlowFromSetInflows came from k distinct payersRoadmap
SolvencyAssets exceed declared liabilitiesRoadmap

FlowAtLeast is the hard one and the valuable one: it turns a solvency check into income verification. Millions of people now earn in stablecoins and cannot prove that income to a lender, a landlord or an embassy without publishing their entire financial life to anyone who asks for a wallet address. FlowFromSet exists because otherwise you game FlowAtLeast by paying yourself in a loop.

What this does not do

A proof-of-concept that oversells itself is worse than one that does not exist, so plainly:

  • No audit. The contracts and circuits have not been reviewed by anyone.
  • The trusted setup is local only. Phase 1 reuses the Hermez ceremony; phase 2 has a single contribution labelled as unsafe for production. A multi-party ceremony is a funded milestone, not a detail.
  • Statements are about now, not the past. The contract reads the ciphertext currently stored in eERC and rejects anything stale. A statement therefore expires the instant the balance moves. Historical attestation needs a commitment accumulator — that is the harder half and it is not built.
  • Revocation cannot un-ring a bell. A subject can burn an unused nonce. Nothing on chain can retract knowledge a verifier already has.
  • Presentation leaks a pattern. The subject’s address, the verifier’s address and the threshold go on chain permanently. Someone who presents balance ≥ 10,000 to five lenders in a week has published a shape, even though no lender saw a figure. Presenting through a relayer address is a v2 fix.

Vouch holds no keys, custodies no funds, and never writes to eERC. If it needed write access to eERC, the design would be wrong.

Status

Reference implementation, written and public: the circuit, the registry contract, the client SDK, an end-to-end demo script that includes a failed replay attempt, and a parity test asserting that the Solidity and TypeScript sides compute the same binding hash. Roughly 500 lines, all of it in the open — and none of it compiled, deployed or audited yet. That is the design made concrete, not a running system.

Next: get it compiling and deployed on Fuji, then adversarial circuit tests, RangeBand, a published SDK, a hosted verify page, the accumulator work behind FlowAtLeast, an independent circuit review, a real ceremony, and C-Chain mainnet.

Built for Avalanche, and only possible there

This cannot be ported. It extends eERC, which exists on Avalanche and nowhere else, and it points at the market Avalanche is winning — tokenized equities, tokenized Treasuries, institutional credit — all of which hit this exact wall the moment a counterparty needs to verify something without seeing a book. The version that matters longest term delivers statements across chains over Interchain Messaging: a bank on a permissioned L1 proving a fact to a counterparty on C-Chain without exposing the chain.

Vouch is additive. It makes every existing eERC application more useful and competes with none of them. If you are building on eERC and need a statement type that does not exist yet, open an issue.
